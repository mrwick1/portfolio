Building a navigation app for my motorcycle

How I studied my motorcycle’s Bluetooth protocol and built an Android navigation app.

Tags
Reverse Engineering Android Bluetooth

My motorcycle’s Bluetooth instrument cluster shows navigation from the manufacturer’s phone app. I wanted to use Google Maps and have more control over the phone-side interface.

I started by observing the connection between my phone and the bike. That work led to REDLINE, an Android app that sends navigation to the existing display.

The process involved packet captures, source inspection, and several corrected assumptions.

IThere Are No Docs

I had no protocol documentation. I used the manufacturer’s compiled app and the traffic from my own phone and bike as references.

A GATT inspection identified the vendor service and its two characteristics: one for phone writes and one for notifications from the bike.

I paired the phone with the bike and captured traffic through Android’s HCI snoop log. This gave me messages to compare with the app’s implementation.

IIReading the App to Read the Protocol

Packet captures showed the traffic, but I still needed to understand the message format. I inspected the APK with JADX and compared the decompiled code with the captures.

I matched captured messages with the code that created them. Frida hooks let me inspect function arguments while the app converted a navigation instruction into bytes.

The messages I decoded used 30-byte frames with a header, message type, body, checksum, and terminator. I found the checksum function in the decompiled code. I also found that the bike waited for a phone write before responding, which explained the empty passive captures.

IIIBeing Wrong, on the Record

Keeping track of incorrect assumptions was as useful as decoding the protocol.

A Bluetooth analyzer labeled the characteristics as a digital-key security specification. Further inspection showed that the label came from UUID matching and did not describe the vendor service I was examining.

I initially assumed the bike had a SIM and uploaded telemetry independently. Checking the hardware and specifications corrected that assumption: this connection depended on the paired phone.

I kept a log of each assumption, the evidence I found, and the correction. This stopped an early guess from becoming the basis for later work.

IVThen I Built the App

REDLINE uses Kotlin and Jetpack Compose. It reads Google Maps navigation notifications and sends the instructions to the cluster over Bluetooth. It also displays telemetry, records rides, exports trips, and shows a clock when navigation is idle.

It's about 14k lines, 205 tests, and runs entirely on the phone with no account and no cloud. The same frame inspector I used to reverse the protocol ships inside the app, because the work isn't really finished.

VWhat It Taught Me

The most useful habit was checking the explanation against the evidence. Tool labels and plausible guesses helped direct the investigation, but they did not establish how the system worked.
